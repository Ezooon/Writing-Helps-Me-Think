“[5 BEST beliefs to have according to neuroscience](https://www.instagram.com/p/DRwWZ0SCuE6/)” was the headline on a reel by [@rewirethemindcoaching](https://www.instagram.com/rewirethemindcoaching?utm_source=ig_web_button_share_sheet&igsh=ZDNlZDc0MzIxNw==) I just watched. I don’t believe I’m getting content from reels now… I want to write about how these beliefs meant to me or how I interpreted them.

I don’t usually put too much weight on these kinds of lessons, because I feel like I’ve already tried everything, but very much like in a video game, the moment you think you’ve tried everything is the moment you really get stuck and can’t move forward, but I dislike long intros so let’s jump to the lessons or beliefs that feel they might work, for the first time in a long time.

# “My emotions are data, not my identity”

Sounds obvious and a little scary, because what if I became detached? What if I started to use even the good emotions as data? I don’t want that, I like myself even if I get paralyzed by my anxiety. I want to feel happy, sad, and angry. Feeling my own feelings is already difficult; I will start crying and stop immediately because, in his words, “your prefrontal cortex activates, and your limbic system deactivates.“

But maybe that’s a good thing. I already know how to do it; in theory, it should be perfect if I can keep the feelings that drive me to myself, and detach from the feelings that paralyze me, like anxiety and fear, I might be able to start moving without losing myself. I will at least give it a try, and maybe you should too; regardless, I’m not letting go of the emotions I finally learned to allow myself to feel for anything.

# “My actions create clarity”

Yeah… It’s your usual intellectualized procrastination and “don’t wait to take action, the right moment will never come” advice. But I like the way he said it, “when you wait for the perfect moment to do something, you lose your ability to actually do action when you say so.”

His words hit the nail on the head, somehow I’ve grown to be comfortable with just knowing that I can do anything; I just need the right time and the right tools, because I know I’m smart and capable (or maybe want to protect the illusion by not really acting).

He used a bunch of neuroscience jargon, but I think the idea is that we need to teach ourselves that action is what matters most. The dopamine releasing form of acting will condition our brains to stop intellectualizing procrastination and prioritize action.

In other words, my words, just act, no matter how small or inconsequential the action is, it’s better than putting things off for later, even if it’s less important than what you really need to get done, you can learn to prioritize latter learn to act first!

# “Pain is neurofeedback, not punishment”

Seems redundant, it’s similar to the first belief, but pain deserves to be discussed separately. Speaking from my own experience, my pain is my failures; no matter what I try, I always fail, whether I give up quickly or bite with my teeth and nails, I always fail.

I assume the neurofeedback here is that I should stop trying because it has been proven that I will fail, just like the hundreds of times before. I wish I could say these are lies my brain is telling me, but they are true. I only have a couple of things that I can call a success on the surface, but they are not when you look deep, like how I graduated with the best score in my class from high school, but it was with 79% score, and how I got this scholarship, but I’m tired of studying I just want to allow myself to enjoy my life, I want to work and make a diffrence I’ve always been studing and yet nothing have changed.

Still, I’m an optimist at heart, and the full half part of the cup is that I proved to myself that my way was better than what the teachers were telling the rest of the class, and I got out of the super restricting living conditions in Sudan to the slightly less restricting Indonesia.

Either way, if my brain will cling to the idea that it’s faliure both ways, then it’s only protecting my pride, even though it’s not worth much. In that case, what I should do is to teach myself to let go of my tiny pride, and that the pain of failing after inaction is greater than the pain of failing to succeed. That’s what I’ll do, I hope you can relate.

# “My identity drives my behaviour, not my willpower”

I somehow read that backwards the first time :3

The idea is, instead of forcing yourself to do something, become the kind of person who does that thing. You’ve probably heard this before: instead of forcing yourself to wake up early every morning, become a person who wakes up early every morning.

It makes sense, because the truth is we spend too much energy trying to protect our identity that we don’t dare to change it, and still force ourselves to act differently, like how I want to have a healthy sleep schedule yet I think of myself as a night owl, and lowkey take pride in staying up late.

Following that logic, we need to let go of the identity that has the bad habits, keep the good ones, and develop a new identity that has better habits. Honsetly it sounds too good to be true, but making light of clearly good advice is a habit my new identity doesn’t have!

# “My brain can change at any age”

I didn’t want to believe this when I heard it. If my brain can change at any age, that means I have no excuse; it means I can learn to read long text and not blame my “undiagnosed ADHD”, and it means I can learn to do my homework on time instead of procrastinating; it means I can learn all these lessons instead of making excuses.

It is scary and uncomfortable knowing you have so much power over the person that is yourself; at the same time, it’s empowering, and personally, I would like to believe that “my brain can change at any age”.
